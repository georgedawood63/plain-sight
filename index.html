The problem

Most buildings fail quietly.

Finding a washroom in an unfamiliar building means reading a sign. Take that away and the building becomes one a person can only use with help, every visit, even somewhere they have been a hundred times.

The barrier is not the space itself. It is what the space costs you in independence.

Signage gets added one piece at a time, over decades, by whoever handled that year’s renovation. Nobody checks the whole building against a standard, because in most public buildings nobody owns signage at all.

That is the job we do.

How a building gets readable

Feel it. See it. Scan it.

Feel it

Braille and tactile lettering on room signs, shelf labels and directories.

See it

Contrast, sign height and type size that hold up across an entire building, not just at the front door.

Scan it

Smart codes that speak the sign aloud, for the many people with sight loss who do not read braille.

Who we work with

Publicly funded. Publicly accountable. No design team.

Large organizations already employ accessibility staff. Schools, libraries and community centres do not.

They have obligations under the AODA and the Ontario Building Code, a real interest in getting this right, and nobody internal to ask.

We are the people to ask.

What we do

Three ways to work with us.

Most organizations start at the top and move down as budget allows.

TIER 1

Audit

We walk your building and check every sign against the current standards: tactile lettering, braille, contrast, mounting height, pictograms and wayfinding logic.

You get a written report with a prioritized fix list, so the urgent items are obvious and the rest can be budgeted over time.

Best for: organizations that know something is wrong but not what.

TIER 2

Specify

We turn the fix list into something a sign shop can produce. Materials, production methods, suppliers and artwork specifications that meet the standards.

No guesswork at the quoting stage, and no signs that have to be remade.

Best for: organizations ready to make changes and buying from outside.

TIER 3

Source & Support

For buildings producing signage regularly, we bring the capability in-house: equipment, installation, staff training and ongoing support.

A new room label should not require a purchase order.

Best for: campuses, library systems and anyone producing signs on demand.

What we are not. We are not a manufacturer, we are not architects, and we are not the right fit for organizations that already run an accessibility team. We are the people who know the rules and know who to call.

Braille basics

What braille is, and what it is not.

Most signage mistakes start with a misunderstanding of how braille works. This page covers what a building owner actually needs to know before ordering a sign.

The cell

Every braille character lives in a cell of six dot positions, two columns of three. Dots are numbered 1, 2 and 3 down the left column and 4, 5 and 6 down the right. Which dots are raised decides the character. Our logo is the braille letter T: dots 2, 3, 4 and 5.

It is read left to right, top to bottom, the same direction as print.

Braille is a code, not a language

This is the most common misunderstanding. Braille does not replace English or French. It is a way of writing a language by touch, so braille follows the grammar and spelling of whatever language it transcribes. A sign in braille is still a sign in English.

Uncontracted and contracted

Uncontracted braille uses roughly one braille character per printed character. Contracted braille adds short forms, closer to shorthand, so it takes less space and is faster for a fluent reader.

These were long called grade 1 and grade 2. The Braille Authority of North America changed the preferred terms to uncontracted and contracted, so modern documents use those words. You will still hear both.

For signage in Canada, uncontracted is the convention, and the International Council on English Braille recommends uncontracted UEB for signage, with contracted allowed only for English sentences longer than ten words. Uncontracted braille also works across English and French, which matters in a bilingual country.

This is where Canada and the United States differ. American signage under the ADA requires contracted braille. Copying an American sign specification into a Canadian building is a real and common error.

Unified English Braille

Canada adopted Unified English Braille in April 2010, the first country in North America to do so. UEB is now used across eight English-speaking countries, which means a visitor who learned braille in Australia can read a sign in Toronto.

Braille Literacy Canada is the recognized braille authority in Canada and publishes accessible signage guidelines.

Indicators: the part people forget

Braille has no separate numerals or capital letters. It uses indicators placed before a character to change how it is read.

  • Numeric indicator before digits. Without it, "214" reads as the letters B, A, D.
  • Capital indicator before a capital letter.
  • Grade 1 indicator where a symbol could otherwise be mistaken for a contraction.

Room numbers are the place this goes wrong most often, which matters because room numbers are most of what a wayfinding system says.

What this means for a sign

  • Braille is placed below the tactile text, in a consistent position so a reader can find it without searching.
  • Dot height and spacing follow a standard, so a braille font is not a typeface choice. Getting the dot profile wrong makes a sign unreadable even when the text is correct.
  • Braille belongs on signs short enough to read by touch. A paragraph of braille on a wall is not useful.
  • Have braille proofread by a braille reader before production. A visual check cannot catch a translation error.

Braille is not the whole answer

Most people with sight loss do not read braille. They are served by contrast, larger type, sign placement and audio. A sign with perfect braille and low contrast grey type still fails most of the people it was meant for, which is why our three pillars are feel it, see it and scan it rather than braille alone.

If you remember one thing: uncontracted Unified English Braille, placed below the tactile text, proofread by someone who reads braille, on a sign that also has strong contrast.
